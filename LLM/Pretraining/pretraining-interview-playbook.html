Q1
"Where does pretraining data come from?"
Your Answer

"There are two main approaches — crawl the web yourself, like OpenAI and Anthropic do, or use a public repository like CommonCrawl. CommonCrawl has been crawling since 2007 and releases a new crawl every one to two months, each containing around 2–3 billion web pages and hundreds of terabytes of HTML. Most open LLM training efforts, including FineWeb, RefinedWeb, and Dolma, start from CommonCrawl and then apply extensive filtering and deduplication."

Mention specific scale — "A single CommonCrawl dump from April 2024 contains 2.7 billion web pages totaling 386 terabytes of uncompressed HTML."
Q2
"How do you define 'high quality' training data?"
Your Answer

"That's actually one of the hardest questions in pretraining — there's no universal definition. Quality depends on the downstream task. There are three main approaches to measure it, each with tradeoffs."

1. Perplexity against a reference corpus (usually Wikipedia) — fast and cheap but unreliable. Biases toward formal text and filters out useful informal content.

2. Train small models and evaluate on diverse benchmarks — more reliable but slower. Critical to use diverse evals and avoid overfitting to any single benchmark.

3. Human evaluation (like LMSYS Chatbot Arena) — most reliable but too expensive for data ablations. Also requires instruction finetuning first.

"The state-of-the-art approach is using an LLM to annotate a small sample for educational quality, then training a cheap classifier on those annotations to filter at scale. This is what Llama 3 and Phi-3 both used — LLM distillation for data curation."
Q3
"Walk me through a pretraining data pipeline."
Your Answer (5-Stage Structure)

1. Text extraction — Starting from raw HTML (WARC) rather than pre-extracted text gives better results. FineWeb showed that using trafilatura on raw HTML produced 25% less data but significantly better performance, because extra tokens were page boilerplate.

2. Base filtering — Remove adult content via URL blocklists, filter to English-only using a language classifier, apply quality and repetition filters. CommonCrawl → ~36T tokens.

3. Deduplication — MinHash for fuzzy dedup within each dump independently. Key finding: cross-dump dedup hurt because good content is duplicated while garbage is unique. Result: 20T tokens.

4. Heuristic filters — Compare metric distributions between clean and dirty data using Wasserstein distance, set thresholds. Three filters removed ~22% and surpassed C4.

5. Educational quality filtering — LLM scores sample, train classifier, filter at scale. Removed 92%, leaving 1.3T tokens that outperformed everything.

End with: "The recurring lesson was that quality matters far more than quantity. They removed 92% of data and performance went up."
Q4
"Tell me about deduplication. Why is it important?"
Your Answer

"Deduplication removes redundant content. The web has mirrors, aggregators, templated pages, and crawler artifacts. Removing duplicates improves model performance, reduces memorization which helps generalization, and increases training efficiency — same compute, more diverse data."

Two types: Exact dedup (identical matches) and Fuzzy dedup (similarity-based, typically MinHash — hash n-grams into buckets, collisions = duplicates).

"One counterintuitive finding from FineWeb: aggressive cross-dump dedup backfired. The oldest dumps lost 90%+ of data, and what remained was worse than what was removed. High-quality content tends to be replicated across the web, while spam is often unique. They switched to individual dump dedup, which fixed it."
Arpit et al. memorization paper — "Duplicated data encourages memorization over learning, which degrades generalization."
Q5
"How would you evaluate whether a data filtering step actually helps?"
Your Answer

"The gold standard is ablation testing. Train two identical models — same architecture, size, tokens, one epoch — with the only difference being whether the filtering step was applied. Compare on diverse benchmarks."

"Benchmarks need low variance across runs, monotonically increasing scores during training, and above-random baseline performance."

"Important caveat: small-scale ablations don't always transfer. FineWeb showed dedup effects were invisible at 28B tokens but clear at 350B. Validate important findings with longer runs."

"You can systematically develop filters by computing 50+ metrics on clean vs dirty data, finding largest Wasserstein distance, setting thresholds from histograms, and validating with ablations. This is a reusable framework for any data quality problem."
Q6
"What is FineWeb-Edu and why does it matter?"
Your Answer

"FineWeb-Edu uses an educational quality classifier on top of FineWeb. The approach: use Llama-3-70B to score 500K samples on educational quality (0–5), train a small embedding model with a regression head on those annotations, then run the classifier on all 15T tokens. With threshold 3, 92% removed, 1.3T tokens remain."

"It outperformed every other open web dataset and achieved same MMLU performance as C4 and Dolma with 10x fewer tokens."

"The pattern — expensive LLM annotates small sample, distill into cheap classifier, run at scale — is called LLM distillation for data curation. Llama 3 and Phi-3 used it but their classifiers aren't public. FineWeb-Edu is the first open reproduction."
Q7
"What about synthetic data in pretraining?"
Your Answer

"The web increasingly contains LLM-generated content. FineWeb tracked ChatGPT-isms like 'delve', 'rich tapestry', 'it's important to note' across crawls. Frequency was flat until mid-2023, then spiked steeply."

"At small training scales, synthetic data doesn't hurt and might help. At large scale, it's an open question — there's a concern called model collapse, where training on AI-generated data degrades future models."

Frame as frontier problem: "This is one of the most important open questions — as the web fills with AI content, how do we ensure future training data quality? No one has a definitive answer yet."

Q9
"What's the Chinchilla scaling law?"
Your Answer

"DeepMind's Chinchilla paper (2022) found that for a given compute budget, there's an optimal ratio of model size to training tokens — roughly 20 tokens per parameter. Before Chinchilla, teams trained huge models on too little data (GPT-3). After, the field shifted toward scaling data proportionally, which is why Llama can compete with much larger models."

FineWeb's ablation models used 28B tokens for 1.82B params — approximately Chinchilla optimal.
Q10
"What's the most important lesson from pretraining data research?"
Your Answer (Memorize This)

"Quality matters far more than quantity. The FineWeb team repeatedly showed this — better text extraction gave 25% less data but better results; individual dedup gave 5x more data than aggressive dedup and performed better because aggressive dedup kept unique garbage and removed common good content; educational filtering removed 92% of data and performance went up. The entire field is moving from 'collect everything' to 'curate carefully.'"

Key Principles (Quick Reference)

  1. Quality >>> Quantity — More data can hurt. 92% removal improved performance.
  2. Uniqueness ≠ Quality — Good content is duplicated because it's good. Garbage is often unique.
  3. Surgical > Aggressive — Smart 10% removal beat blunt 30% removal.
  4. Validate every step — Ablation test each decision. Don't assume.
  5. Small-scale ≠ Large-scale — 28B results don't always hold at 350B+.
  6. LLM distillation for curation — Expensive LLM → cheap classifier → scale. State of the art.
  7. Balance over maximization — Every technique has diminishing returns.
